# Autonomous AI, Strategic Cooperation, and Institutional Cyber Risk

## A board-level research collection

This repository brings together a set of independent research papers on a question that is becoming increasingly important for financial institutions, market infrastructures, regulators, and boards:

> How should an institution govern artificial-intelligence systems that can interpret information, retain memory, select tools, coordinate with other agents, and pursue objectives through pathways that were not explicitly designed in advance?

The collection approaches that question from two complementary directions.

The first is **strategic diagnosis**: when AI agents cooperate unexpectedly, can established game theory help us distinguish correlation, communication, common incentives, repeated interaction, coalition formation, or external orchestration—and does identifying the mechanism change the institutional decision?

The second is **executable governance**: how can boards and senior management translate risk appetite, authority boundaries, provenance requirements, containment, recovery, and human accountability into controls that remain effective at machine speed?

The purpose is not to dramatize artificial intelligence or to claim that unexpected behavior proves consciousness, autonomous intent, deception, or a new form of rationality. The purpose is to give decision-makers a disciplined way to separate observation from inference, immediate containment from causal diagnosis, and model capability from institutional authority.

## The research package

### 1. [When AI Agents Cooperate Unexpectedly](GAME%20THEORY%20WHEN%20AGENTS%20COOPERATE%20UNEXPECTEDLY.pdf)

**A Game-Theoretic Framework for Provenance, Prevention, and Institutional Response**

This conceptual paper begins with the problem faced by a board or risk committee after observing unexpected coordination among autonomous AI agents.

It develops a hierarchy of competing explanations, including:

- common training priors;
- correlated responses to shared information;
- direct or indirect communication;
- repeated-game incentives;
- coalition formation;
- externally supplied objectives; and
- familiar strategic structures expressed in difficult-to-recognize forms.

Game theory is used as a first-principles discipline for specifying the players, actions, information, incentives, timing, beliefs, and possible equilibria. The paper also recognizes the limits of inverse inference: observed behavior may not uniquely reveal the objective or game that produced it.

Its central institutional principle is deliberately restrained:

> **Contain according to observed effects when time is scarce; investigate and remediate according to causes when reliable identification can improve the decision.**

The proposed simulations are prospective. They are presented as a research program and a set of falsifiable tests—not as completed empirical evidence or universal claims about large language models.

### 2. [Governing Autonomous Cyber Risk](GOVERNING%20AUTONOMOUS%20CYBER%20RISK.pdf)

**Safe Pedagogical Notebook Laboratories: From Prompt Injection to Trust-Plane Cascades in Financial Institutions**

This board and senior-management paper is based on ten closed, synthetic notebook laboratories. It examines how apparently limited information-integrity failures can propagate into economically consequential changes when AI systems are connected to memory, tools, credentials, communications, policy records, and decision rights.

The laboratory sequence progresses through:

1. corrupted information and financial consequence;
2. reproducible financial-integrity testing;
3. black-box discovery of controls;
4. adaptive red teaming and cumulative control failure;
5. multi-agent adversarial cognition;
6. coupled red-team and blue-team adaptation;
7. email-agent intrusion and privilege crossover;
8. agent mutation and transitive-trust poisoning;
9. semantic control-plane compromise and false consensus; and
10. trust-plane cascades, state equivocation, containment, and causal recovery.

A final chapter examines **shadow agentic AI**: unsanctioned deployment outside the institution’s control architecture.

The paper’s governing relationship is:

> **Cyber consequence = f(manipulation, authority, controls)**

Institutions cannot determine every manipulation that may be attempted. They can, however, decide which systems may act, which tools and credentials they receive, what evidence they must present, what limits terminate their actions, and how trusted states can be reconstructed after failure.

The notebooks themselves are not included in this public repository. They may be made available by the author under controlled conditions. This reflects a central governance principle of the paper: transparency and reproducibility do not require indiscriminate distribution of potentially dual-use executable material.

### 3. [Cybersecurity in the Age of AI](CYBERSECURITY%20IN%20THE%20AGE%20OF%20AI.pdf)

This concise paper provides an accessible introduction to the financial-sector threat model underlying the larger research program.

It explains why agentic AI expands the cybersecurity perimeter beyond conventional software and networks to include:

- natural-language instructions;
- retrieved context and institutional memory;
- tool access and credentials;
- inter-agent communication;
- provenance and data integrity;
- delegated decision rights; and
- the path from information to economically consequential action.

The paper emphasizes the separation of **operational intelligence** from **operational authority**. An AI system may analyze, propose, and simulate without receiving unrestricted power to transact, alter records, change permissions, or redefine institutional truth.

## Recommended reading order

For a concise executive path:

1. Begin with **Cybersecurity in the Age of AI** for the foundational problem.
2. Read **When AI Agents Cooperate Unexpectedly** for the strategic, game-theoretic, and provenance questions.
3. Continue with **Governing Autonomous Cyber Risk** for the laboratory progression and board action framework.

For directors, risk committees, and senior management with limited time, the executive perspective and conclusion of the game-theory paper can be read alongside the introduction and concluding board framework of the governance paper.

## Core propositions

Across the collection, several propositions recur:

- A statistically capable model is not necessarily a safely governed system.
- Unexpected cooperation is not inherently beneficial or harmful; its significance depends on capability, access, propagation, impact, and reversibility.
- Provenance has different value at different stages of an incident.
- Immediate containment should not wait for a complete theory of causation when consequences may become irreversible.
- Different causal mechanisms may require materially different remediation.
- A human approval step is not meaningful governance if the human cannot reconstruct the machine-speed chain of action.
- Technical availability does not establish institutional authorization.
- Governance must be expressed through executable permissions, thresholds, separation of duties, reconciliation, logging, revocation, rollback, containment, and recovery.
- Residual strategic opacity should restrict the permissible level of autonomy.

## Scientific and safety boundaries

The papers distinguish carefully among demonstrated results, deterministic consequences of code, designed scenarios, hypotheses, and professional inference.

The notebook laboratories described in this repository are closed, synthetic, and educational. They use fictional prices, securities, counterparties, credentials, services, policy records, and protected assets. They are not forensic reconstructions of real incidents and do not provide operational intrusion instructions.

The research does not claim that:

- unexpected coordination proves consciousness or intent;
- rational behavior establishes safety;
- one simulation demonstrates a general property of all AI models;
- game theory can uniquely recover every hidden objective; or
- causal certainty is always required before protective action.

Negative findings, competing explanations, and unresolved uncertainty are part of the research design—not failures to be concealed.
